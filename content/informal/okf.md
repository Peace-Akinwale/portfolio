## Portfolio summary

An OKF (Open Knowledge Format) is a folder of short notes, one idea per note. Each note records where it came from, whether a person checked it, and when it goes stale. I turned an SEO agency founder's 1.23 million words of notes into an OKF of 683 linked notes. The team can ask it questions and get answers that cite the exact notes they came from. Then I built a kit that does the same for a client's own documents, which the client hosts themselves.

## Case study

### What an OKF is

Open Knowledge Format stores knowledge as short, linked text notes that people and AI tools can both read. Each note records its sources and its review status, with a date for checking whether it's still current. That gives a team a lot more context than an answer copied out of an unlabelled document.

### The founder's knowledge was scattered, and the team kept asking

The agency's founder had years of thinking spread across two Notion archives, an AI project and their own head. When the team needed an answer, they asked the founder. Or they asked an AI tool connected to the raw archives, which searched whole pages and gave patchy answers. The goal was one distilled source the team could ask instead, in the founder's own frameworks.

### 1.23 million words into 683 linked notes

I turned about 1.23 million words, across more than 500 pages, into focused notes, including concepts and playbooks. Related notes link to each other, so a teammate can move from an explanation to the procedure it supports. The reviewed snapshot had 683 notes with 2,939 links.

### Ask a question, and get an answer you can check

A teammate can ask how the agency handles a particular piece of work, instead of searching the archives or waiting for the founder. The tool uses an index to pick up to eight relevant notes, then reads them in full to put together an answer grounded in that material.

[[img]]

I made source citations a requirement, along with a clear marker for outside material. A teammate can open a cited note and check the instructions before using them. Its review status shows whether someone has checked the note, or whether it still needs a person to look it over.

[[img]]

### Review proposed notes before they're published

New material can come from a URL, a Notion page, a YouTube video, a PDF or pasted text. Before anything gets published, the tool screens it for secrets and personal data, pulls out proposed notes and shows a plan. The plan says which notes it'll create, which it'll update and which links it'll add. An admin approves or rejects it. And if a publish turns out wrong, one click adds a reversing change, so nothing ever gets quietly rewritten.

[[img]]

I made the review step mandatory early on. The first version published new material automatically. After watching a long video turn into notes I hadn't seen, I switched it to plan-then-approve, so I could see exactly what was being added before it landed.

### Keep “verified” for notes a person has reviewed

A person has to approve a note before it gets verified status. Existing notes started out unverified, instead of getting that label automatically. That helps teammates tell what they can use with confidence from what they should check before acting on it.

### Build a knowledge bundle the client hosts

The agency then wanted to offer this to clients. I designed the client version so the client hosts it themselves and covers the hosting costs. A strategist or the client drops in files, a folder or a zip, and gets back a linked note bundle, a map and a website they can host.

[[img]]

A colleague has already used the same skill to build a client bundle. Export checks look for agency keys and server addresses, which helps keep internal access details out of a package handed to a client.

### What the build delivered

I directed the build with AI coding agents. The aim was to make the agency's knowledge usable when its key people weren't around, while keeping review decisions with people. Teammates can ask for guidance and check the material behind an answer before they act on it.
